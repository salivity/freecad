FreeCAD FEM Contact Constraints Explained

Contact constraints in the FreeCAD Finite Element Method (FEM) workbench are specialized boundary conditions used to simulate mechanical interactions between separate, touching parts. This article explains the primary function of contact constraints, how they handle non-linear structural behavior, and why they are essential for obtaining accurate stress and displacement results in multi-body assemblies.

In finite element analysis, parts modeled as distinct solids will naturally pass through one another under applied loads unless instructed otherwise. The primary purpose of a contact constraint is to prevent this non-physical penetration. It defines an interface between two distinct geometric surfaces, designated as a "master" and a "slave" surface, enforcing physical boundaries where bodies meet.

When an external load pushes these bodies together, the contact constraint detects the proximity of the mesh nodes and generates reaction forces along the contact normal. This enables realistic load transfer from one component to another without fusing them into a single continuous mesh. Unlike bonded connections or boolean unions, which permanently fix surfaces together, contact constraints allow surfaces to separate naturally if the applied forces pull them apart.

Contact constraints also model tangential behavior between parts. Depending on the solver configuration—typically CalculiX within FreeCAD—the constraint can simulate either frictionless contact or friction-based interaction. In frictionless mode, components are free to slide along each other laterally while still resisting normal penetration. When friction is enabled, the constraint opposes relative sliding based on a defined friction coefficient, accurately representing mechanical conditions like press fits, clamped flanges, and sliding bearings.

Because contact conditions can change dynamically as loads increase and parts deform, introducing contact constraints transforms a simulation into a non-linear problem. The solver must iteratively calculate whether nodes are touching, sliding, or separating. Despite the increased computational cost, using contact constraints is necessary whenever you need to evaluate local contact pressures, avoid over-constraining an assembly, or capture true mechanical behavior between interacting components.
